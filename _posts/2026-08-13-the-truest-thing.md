# The Truest Thing

The porter takes the pound notes without counting. A four-hundred-year-old castle teaches its staff not to ask why a woman wants into a room that isn't hers.

"The bride and groom won't be needing it," I tell him, before he can ask anyway.

He doesn't. Just turns the key twice, the old kind, iron heavy enough to bruise a knuckle, and leaves me to the room the brochure calls the Tower Suite.

It is, I'll give them that, built for exactly what it's supposed to be. A four-poster hung with deep claret curtains, gone almost black where the firelight doesn't reach. Rose petals scattered across the counterpane that morning, already curling brown at the edges by the time I availed myself the opportunity. A fire someone lit hours ago and left to do its own thing, warm on one side of my face, cool on the other. Two robes on the hooks by the door, white, monogrammed, a P and an S stitched into the corner. The bed had been turned down on both sides, a chocolate going soft on each pillow. A magnum sweat itself flat in a silver bucket by the window, losing its ice by the hour, nobody left to drink it. Save for yours truly, of course.

I don't take off my coat for a long while. Gloves too, the ring loose inside my closed fist, warm from being held too long, worth more than you could possibly imagine.

You want to know how a woman ends up in a room built for two, holding a stranger's wedding ring, alone, an untended fire dying in the grate.

Fine. Start at the tie.

Scott doesn't wear a tie so much as suffer and endure it. Seven attempts that morning, each one undone the instant his fingers found the half-centimeter it sat off-square against his collarbone. Nobody else in the room could have measured, let alone noticed. He rebuilt the knot from scratch each time rather than adjust what was already there. I did it for him on attempt eight, my knuckles against his throat, his pulse jumping once under them and then going flat and even.

By the time the family filed through to wish him well, none of that anxiety showed. He had a joke ready for each of them, pitched exact to what that particular person needed to hear, a hand gripped half a second longer for the ones who needed convincing. In that room he was the easiest, warmest man anyone had ever come to meet.

Billie filled the doorway in her catering blacks, sleeves pinned back, a tray balanced flat on one forearm. The water glasses were sweating rings onto the linen beside a teapot she had no real reason to be carrying this far from the kitchens. Her eyes found mine in the mirror before they found his. She winked.

"You're not needed till the toast," I told her.

She crossed the room, set the tray down harder than the china wanted, one glass ringing against another. "I know what I'm needed for." Then she left before I could read her face.

I found the bride an hour later in a dressing room the size of a New York flat. There was a gilt mirror spotted with age, an ivory gown hung from the picture rail, every fold pinned into place, and a window box of white roses forced to bloom out of season for the occasion.

I missed the start of it. Caught the aftermath instead: a tray of water and toast gone cold on the vanity, whatever excuse Billie had used abandoned the second the door closed. Poppy's bridal slip pulled sloppily off one shoulder, hairpins scattered across the vanity, more of them lost than left in her hair. Billie's shirt untucked at the waist, the collar of it damp, her lipstick smeared. Both of them still breathing hard.

I managed a cough and they broke apart. Nobody explained anything. Billie left without looking at me.

Poppy's face had colour in it for the first time all morning, high on both cheekbones, eyes anywhere but the mirror, hands finding a handkerchief on the vanity and working it.

Her father, she said, once she had breath back for words. Her father had picked the flowers. Her father had picked the date. Her father would be standing where her mother should've stood, and nobody in this whole enormous place had asked her a single question about any of it, including whether she wanted to get married at all.

"Time to get you ready, darling," I said, sweet and flat, and ushered her toward the box on the vanity. Black velvet, worn bald at the corners, the ring inside caught what little light came through the leaded glass. Some tradition the family swore was centuries old, blessing it with incense before it went on her finger.

I didn't need long. Her eyes lingered on the door Billie had gone through. Not on the box, not on my hands moving inside it. One motion, cold metal for cold metal. The real ring went into the lining of my coat before the incense had finished smoking itself out, the copy I'd carried since London pressed into the velvet, filling the same hollow. Convincing enough for a bride who wasn't looking, which for once worked entirely in my favour.

The ceremony ran the way ceremonies run in houses like that. A hall built for siege councils, hung now with white orchids flown in from somewhere that wasn't Wales, a laminated fire-exit map tucked behind a suit of armour nobody had dusted this century. The mass was too long with too much Latin. A harpist worked overtime against a half-millennia of drafts coming through arrow-slit glazed windows. Scott stood at the altar with his practiced stage face, easy, warm, the perfect picture of the groom two hundred people had paid to see. Billie and I both knew some other part of him logged the harpist drifting flat on every third bar and the flower arrangements were placed two inches off symmetry. His tell was how still his hands went, fingers laced behind his back until the knuckles paled. He'd wait the ceremony out rather than live inside it.

They said their I do's. Poppy's hand shook getting the band onto Scott's finger, an ordinary ring, exactly what it looked like. Scott's hand didn't shake at all placing hers. He kept on task.

Poppy glanced back at the assembled crowd and her eyes found Billie somewhere past the second row, the same face she'd had her hands in an hour before, composed now, giving nothing away. Before the priest could say the last of it, she pulled her ring back off.

Not gently. Wrenched it, knuckle catching for real this time, and closed her fist around it before it could hit the floor. She was already in flight by the time anyone understood a thing, down the aisle the wrong direction, past two hundred people in furs and rented kilts, past an ice sculpture already weeping onto its silver tray.

Nobody found her in the forty minutes I stood at the edge of the reception marquee, a glass of champagne going flat and abandoned in my hand, the search fracturing into search parties, into arguments, into her father shouting at men in earpieces who didn't work for him. Scott stood very still through all of it, apart from the crowd, a coin from his pocket walking across his knuckles and back, over and over, the same flawless pass he'd use to steady a room full of strangers. I didn't go to him. There wasn't any version of the premise where either of us came out looking like what and who we were supposed to be.

My own eyes found Billie instead, refilling glasses nobody was drinking from, her face giving away nothing to anyone. In the middle of all of it, she caught my glance and touched two fingers to her own collar, quick, straightening something that wasn't there. I nodded.

So I did the only useful thing left to do. Found the porter and told him just enough. This wasn't where I was supposed to end the night. But improvising was the whole job, and some nights... Well, I'd learned a long time ago not to waste a room that was already paid for.

I wrestle out of my evening wear, indulge a shower, wrap myself in one of the robes. Pour two glasses from the magnum nobody's coming to drink and carry both to the window, where the last of the grey has settled into full dark over the hills, and drink them standing up. The second robe stays on its hook, white and pointless, going dim as the fire burns down behind it. Somewhere below me, an orchestra booked through midnight keeps playing to a room that's mostly emptied out.

I put the ring on, at some point, alone by the fire, and don't take it off again until the fire has burned all the way down.

Somewhere past midnight, long after the orchestra finally gives up, my phone lights the dark.

Not his name.

*Did I ruin it,* Billie has written, and nothing else, like she doesn't know the answer and thinks I might.

I look at it a long moment, firelight catching the stolen ring still cold on my finger, before I answer.

*Come here, lover,* I write back. Nothing else. It's the truest thing I've said all day.
