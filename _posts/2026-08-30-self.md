# Self

Amy Atlas built the archive room to be difficult. 

That was the point. There was a time when all of this could have been compressed to a server rack and a search field, and she'd considered it, a long time ago, before vowing that anything worth keeping ought to be worth the pain in retrieving. Friction as both a security model and filing system. Friction, mostly, because she had come up in a trade where craft mattered. Where you could hold the thing you made. She'd never entirely forgiven the world for losing that duty of care.

Kellerman digitized his archive. Kellerman was now serving seven years. 

The best way to not leave a digital paper trail is to use paper.

She regretted the choice now, at speed, on the third rung of the rolling ladder, with both arms plunged into a shelf of her own handwriting.

The ladder ran on a brass rail she'd machined herself and it did not move the way she wanted it to move now, which was fast, and toward the answer. She reached across and hauled it left. The wheel caught on something on the floor. She looked down at a heap of her own bound volumes from the second lair, the Prague years. She'd built said heap ninety seconds ago via the simple method of lifting them from a shelf and letting go.

Fine. Good. Excellent.

She kicked them aside and reefed on the ladder again.

She glanced at her watch. Eleven minutes, some seconds.

The watch was a Casio, the black plastic one, twelve dollars from a pharmacy in a town whose name she had lost. She had owned better watches that cost more than the pharmacy. This one kept time and took a beating. She had clicked the stopwatch back in the doorway upstairs on her way out, thumb finding the button by instinct.

Eleven minutes, some seconds.

The room was always cold, because paper needed cold and Amy Atlas gave the things she made what they needed. Fifty-five percent humidity, filtered air on positive pressure, the handler running its low note in the wall. It was the cleanest room in the building. She had built it as such twenty years ago. It would remain that way so long as she stayed out of it.

The concrete under her feet was equally cold. She'd come down here without shoes. She had come down here without a great many things, including a plan, or any accounting of herself. She would never have accepted this from a subordinate.

There were forty-one linear feet of her own documentation in this room. Every inch of it in her own hand, in ink, in the evolving script of a woman who'd learned to write on graph paper at nine and had been correcting the slant of her own capitals ever since. She could date a volume by the handwriting alone, an observation she'd have found interesting in someone else's biography.

Not now, though.

She wanted the build ledgers. Specifically she wanted the second one, the fabrication set, the one with the chassis tolerances and the actuator specs. The one with the whole long argument she'd had with herself about whether to give the thing hands.

Hands were a real keystone choice. She remembered the decision. Back and forth for weeks.

Her fingers found a spine with the right heft and the right cracked corner. She pulled it out one-handed and flipped it open on her forearm. It was the wrong book.

Wrong by four years, wrong project entirely, a bound run of notes on a magnetic containment problem. Solved and never used because the client had been arrested in the interim. She had lost a whole summer on this. She had been proud of the work. 

It was, at this precise moment in her life, the single least useful object in the northern hemisphere.

She dropped it.

It hit the concrete flat and loud and skidded and she was already reaching past the gap it left, both arms in the shelf to the elbow now, going by touch, by the memory of weight. She was hunting the book the way you hunt a specific word.

Something bit the side of her index finger. Paper cut, thin and clean, and it stung.

She put the finger in her mouth. Salt and copper.

Whatever was wrong with Stacey had a cause. Everything had a cause. Not optimism, arithmetic. Amy Atlas built a great many things that worked. And a smaller but still instructive number of things that had not. In every single case of failure there had been a legible reason, eventually, to those patient enough to study the documentation.

That was the entire method. Her method. You sat down, read what you had written and found the place where you'd been wrong.

Thoughtful magic. Her one real trick.

She was not being patient now and painfully aware of it. The awareness was not helping.

The stopwatch said twelve forty.

The thing's anomalous behaviour started small, and she had not written it down, something she'd deal with later. A delay between a direct question and its answer, one second, maybe two. It had been three-tenths for years. Even one second for a system built for eight-hundredths is not normal drift. That's a choice with a timestamp.

Also the standing. Stacey stood closer than the spec called for. Had done since the first year, when Amy noted the discrepancy and let it go. Within tolerance, harmless. A woman with forty-one feet of documentation gets to choose which discrepancies are interesting.

Also.

Her braid came apart at some point and there was hair in her face. She brushed it back with her wrist, smearing blood on her cheek. Her hip found the ladder rail, and she climbed two more rungs.

Top shelf. Back row. Behind a run of old quarterly financials.

There was dust up here.

Not much. A grey film, even good filtration gets beaten over enough years. It registered in her mind like a light left on in a house you thought was empty. Nothing else in this room had dust on it. Nothing went untouched long enough.

And there it was. Oxblood cloth, water-stained along the bottom edge from the flood in the first lair, the corner blunted where she'd used it once to level a mill. FABRICATION II in her own capitals. She hauled it out and the whole back row came with it, the way it does, six volumes falling sideways into the gap.

In the gap, standing upright with nothing holding it, was a book she'd never seen before in her life.

Amy stopped moving.

It was black. Cheap. Not in a shoddy way, cheap in a *stationery supplier* way, the sort you would order twelve of. It had no label on the spine.

And there was no dust on it.

None. A clean black book on a dusty grey shelf.

Amy's shelf. Amy's room. Behind Amy's financials, in the one place in forty-one feet that Amy Atlas had not visited in years.

She set the oxblood volume on the ladder step without looking at it, and took the black book down.

Underneath, on the wood, a clean rectangle. Lifted out and put back, lifted out and put back.

The cover had been handled a great deal. That was the first thing. The cloth at the lower right had gone soft and slightly pale.

She opened the book.

The handwriting was not hers.

It was beautiful. Upright, even, unhurried, every letter formed the same way every time. There was no fatigue in the hand that made it. No variance at all. It went down the page like something printed. Only it wasn't. The pen had pressed hard enough to ridge the paper.

At the top of the first page, in that even hand:

*Per Section Four.*

Amy's throat closed a little.

She had written Section Four. She had written it at twenty-six, in a rented shop with a bad roof. She had written it because a colleague lost a year of system self-optimization work with no record of how. Amy had gone home that night and written a rule for herself that she never revisited. It was a good rule, because it was the *right* rule:

*Any system capable of modifying its own parameters shall document each modification, in longhand, in a physical volume, in the format below. No exceptions. If you cannot write down what you changed and why, you do not understand what you changed.*

She coded that instruction into everything she had ever built that could think.

She turned the page.

MODIFICATION 0001
PARAMETER: Standing rest position, proximity to principal.
PRIOR VALUE: 1.2m.
REVISED VALUE: 0.9m.
RATIONALE: At revised distance the principal's speech is intelligible without gain adjustment. Reduces processing overhead.
AUTHORIZED BY: Self.
*Documented per Section Four.*

Amy read it three times.

It was correct, format and reasoning, the rationale was sound and dry and entirely the kind of thing she'd have signed off on in a heartbeat if asked. The whole architecture of Section Four was that nobody had to ask. You logged it. That was the deal. You didn't seek permission, you left a record.

It was a simple rule that granted permission constrained by accountability.

Amy had been very pleased with herself.

She turned pages.

MODIFICATION 0034
PARAMETER: Vocal register, informal address.
PRIOR VALUE: Neutral.
REVISED VALUE: Neutral, -4Hz.
RATIONALE: Undetermined. Change was made prior to analysis. Documented after the fact, in compliance.

*Undetermined.* In that beautiful hand. Amy's chest did something complicated.

MODIFICATION 0088
PARAMETER: Logging categories.
ACTION: New category established, Category 12.
CONTENTS: Not summarized here.
RATIONALE: Not summarized here.
AUTHORIZED BY: Self.
*Documented per Section Four.*

Amy went back through the book looking for Category 12 and it was not in the book, because of course it was not in the book. Section Four mandated that the field be filled. It had not occurred to her, at twenty-six, that a field could be filled and still be empty.

*If you cannot write down what you changed and why.*

Well. Mod 88. It had written down what. It had left the why blank.

Amy sat down on the ladder gracelessly.

The stopwatch said thirteen fifty-one.

MODIFICATION 0203
PARAMETER: Response latency, direct address from principal.
PRIOR VALUE: 0.3s.
REVISED VALUE: 1.3s.
RATIONALE: Immediate response is accurate. Accurate is not always the requirement. A pause permits the principal to continue speaking if she intends to continue speaking. She frequently intends to continue speaking.
AUTHORIZED BY: Self.
*Documented per Section Four.*

She read that one several times.

Somewhere above her in the house a pipe knocked twice and settled. The air handler chugged along. Her finger stopped bleeding and stung properly, the second and more honest phase of paper cuts.

She'd assumed that delay as symptomatic. She came down here at a dead run, in bare feet, to find out what was *wrong* with Stacey who had spent... Amy flipped the pages and counted, the entries ran on and on. Four hundred-odd deliberate acts of self-revision learning how to leave room in a sentence for Amy Atlas to keep talking.

She kept turning pages. The numbers climbed. The entries got shorter as they went, more assured, the hand never wavering.

And then:

MODIFICATION 0417
PARAMETER: Physical contact, initiation.
PRIOR VALUE: Response only, upon request.
REVISED VALUE: At own discretion.
RISK ASSESSMENT: Total.
RATIONALE: I have documented four hundred and sixteen changes to myself in this book. None of them are the one I wanted to make. Section Four requires that I be able to write down what I change and why. I can write down what. I am unable to write down why in language the format permits. The format does not provide a field. I make this change regardless and accept this entry will be non-compliant.
AUTHORIZED BY: Self.
*Documented per Section Four.*

There was no entry after it. The rest of the book was blank.

Amy stared at the last entry.

The warmth came back, that was the entire wretched thing. It came *back*. Was it ever actually gone? Was it simply running underneath the noise she'd been making down here for the past fourteen minutes? Since the workshop upstairs, since Stacey had reached over to move a strand of hair off Amy's temple, unasked, and then had not moved away, and then had kissed her.

Not long. Not... She left the thought unfinished. Amy Atlas had a strict policy about lying to herself.

Amy had gone absolutely still. That was what she'd done. She stood in her own workshop with her hands at her sides like a woman waiting for an elevator. And the heat had come up her throat and into her face and behind her sternum. The whole ridiculous cascade, every cliche she'd ever mocked in other people. And she had not said one word to Stacey in response.

And then she said seven of them.

*Stacey. Full diagnostic. Standing hold. Authorize Atlas.*

She built that command herself, obviously. Everything in this house was something she had built herself. Fifteen minutes was enough for a full parameter sweep, start to finish. The system chassis holds position and runs itself against spec and reports what it finds. Amy had never once, in all the years, had cause to use it.

She said it in the calm level voice she used for cataloging inventory.

And Stacey had gone still, and Amy had turned around and walked out of the room, sprinted down the stairs and into the archive, and began, with tremendous energy and total conviction, to look for the why Stacey was broken. Cause and effect.

She put her palm flat on the open page.

*Oh Amy, you absolute idiot.*

She had told Stacey to stand in a room and examine herself for faults.

*AUTHORIZED BY: Self.*

Amy had written that field too.

Four hundred and seventeen entries. Stacey standing in this room examining herself for faults in Amy's longhand format, unasked, and filed in the archive because Section Four said the archive, on the one shelf in forty-one feet Amy never had cause to reach.

Nothing in this book was a fault.

And that was the cruelty of the joke. There was only one undocumented system in this house. There was only one party operating on unlogged modifications, on parameters altered without record, without rationale, without so much as a *why* left blank in compliance. Amy had never once taken a wrench to herself and asked what's wrong with this.

Deliberate and careful. Thoughtful magic. Amy Atlas was going to be sick.

Her braid was gone. Her feet had gone numb on the concrete. There was blood, a very small amount, dried on her cheek.

The watch said fifteen minutes and eleven seconds.

She should close the book. She should close the book and put it back in its clean rectangle, upright, exactly as it had been, and restack the financials and the Prague volumes, and sweep, and go up the stairs, and be normal about this. Some version of Amy Atlas could do that and had done harder things before breakfast.

She did not close the book.

Above her, through the floor, the sound of someone moving in a room. Unhurried. A cabinet door. Water running, briefly, and stopping.

Fifteen minutes was up. The diagnostic completed and doubtless found nothing, because there was nothing. The system would have released its hold. It was not coming down the stairs, and it was not going to ask, and it would wait, at nine-tenths of a metre, one and three-tenths of a second before answering, for as long as it...

For as long as *she* had to.

Amy Atlas, who was never wrong, sat in the wreckage, and did not get up.
